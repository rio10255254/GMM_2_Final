Measurement-Oriented Microscopy Reconstruction for 
Cross-Magnification Micro-Displacement Inspection 
Yen Hsuan Chianga , Liang-Chia Chena* 
a Department of Mechanical Engineering, National Taiwan University, No.1, Section 4, Roosevelt Rd., Taipei City, 10617, Taiwan 
*Corresponding author/Email: lchen@ntu.edu.tw 
ABSTRACT 
Image reconstruction for automated optical inspection must improve 
visibility without converting optical variation, sampling phase, or 
learned sharpening into false motion. This paper presents MOSR, a 
measurement-oriented same-size microscopy reconstruction method 
designed around three metrology failure modes: field- and 
magnification-dependent 
optical 
variation, 
sampling-phase 
sensitivity, and reconstruction-induced geometry drift. MOSR 
addresses them through spatial-variation-aware point-spread function 
(PSF) guidance, anti-aliased shift-stable reconstruction, and an input-
anchored geometry-preserving output. A single set of MOSR weights 
processes 20x and 50x images with magnification-specific calibrated 
optical information. Raw, fixed Wiener, and MOSR share one 
threshold-free maximum-gradient and least-squares circle (LSC) 
measurement backend. Across four sequences and 120 predefined 
adjacent-frame pairs, MOSR reduced step standard deviation, 
nominal-step mean absolute error, and median within-grid SD by 
17.9%, 18.0%, and 61.0% relative to Raw, and by 7.8%, 10.6%, and 
18.2% relative to fixed Wiener. These results demonstrate improved 
native-grid positional stability across objective changes without 
redefining the downstream measurement. 
Keywords-Automated optical inspection; measurement-oriented 
reconstruction; cross-magnification; micro-displacement; positional 
stability. 
I. INTRODUCTION 
Image-based optical metrology derives dimensional information 
from optical image responses and edge-related signals [1]. Periodic 
circular features such as via- and bump-like arrays are commonly 
localized during automated optical inspection. When displacement is 
measured from reconstructed micrographs, reconstruction becomes 
part of the measurement chain: a visually sharper edge can still move 
by a fraction of a pixel and change the reported center. Positional 
stability is therefore required in addition to apparent resolution. 
Learning-based microscopy restoration can recover structures that 
are difficult to inspect in raw data [2]. U-Net-type encoder-decoders 
combine contextual feature extraction with precise localization [3], 
but commonly used downsampling operations can introduce shift 
sensitivity [4]. The problem intensifies when one inspection 
workflow spans 20x overview images and 50x verification images, 
because object-space sampling, optical response, and pixel phase 
change with the objective. 
Rather than maximizing visual sharpness, MOSR is organized 
around three metrology failure modes. Spatial-variation-aware PSF 
guidance addresses optical variation across field positions and 
magnifications. Anti-aliased, shift-stable reconstruction is designed 
to reduce sampling-phase sensitivity. An input-anchored geometry-
preserving output limits reconstruction-induced boundary and center 
drift. A single set of model weights processes both magnifications 
with their corresponding calibrated optical information. 
The contribution is therefore not a new visual-quality objective 
alone. It is a coordinated reconstruction strategy in which optical 
guidance constrains correction direction, shift-stable processing 
limits pixel-phase effects, and the input anchor retains the original 
geometric reference. The method is evaluated only after these 
controls are connected to one fixed subpixel measurement backend. 
II. METHOD 
A. Metrology-driven MOSR design 
MOSR receives one monochrome frame and returns a same-size 
frame on the native coordinate grid. First, calibrated optical 
information from multiple field locations forms spatial-variation-
aware guidance, supplying a bounded optical direction rather than 
replacing the input by strong inverse filtering. Second, anti-aliased 
feature expansion and translation-consistency learning are used to 
reduce position changes caused by decoder sampling. Third, polarity-
aware bounded boundary correction is combined with an input 
anchor so that large-scale intensity, low-frequency geometry, and the 
original center reference remain tied to the measured frame. Figure 1 
summarizes the three design groups and the common measurement 
backend. 
One shared set of MOSR weights processes both 20x and 50x 
inputs; the optical information changes with the calibrated objective, 
but the learned reconstruction weights and native-grid output 
convention remain fixed. A fixed Wiener baseline, based on Wiener 
filtering [5], uses magnification-specific calibrated PSFs with one 
regularization setting fixed for all frames. 
These components are designed to reduce sampling-phase 
sensitivity and geometry drift, and their effects are assessed through 
the common boundary-and-center backend.

Profiles are sampled along its outward normals, and the correct-
polarity gradient maximum is refined to a subpixel position by local 
quadratic interpolation [7]. These points, rather than the regularized 
active-contour pixels, define the measured boundary. 
Let (xi, yi) denote the accepted points. A geometric least-squares 
circle (LSC) [8] supplies the center and radius by minimizing the 
sum of squared radial residuals; stage commands and crop 
initialization do not enter the fit. The common estimator was verified 
with synthetic circles, known translations, and perturbed crop 
initializations before comparison. 
For each frame pair, the median of nine pillar shifts represents the 
array step, while their standard deviation defines the within-grid SD. 
Aggregate Step SD and nominal MAE are the means of the four 
condition-level values; Median within-grid SD is the median of the 
120 pair-level nine-pillar standard deviations. The three criteria 
remain separate rather than being combined into an adjustable score. 
III. EXPERIMENT AND RESULTS 
A. Locked cross-magnification experiment 
Four displacement sequences were recorded from a periodic 3x3 
pillar array using 20x and 50x objectives at nominal 0.5 and 1.0 
micrometer steps. Their object-space sampling intervals were 0.1725 
and 0.0690 micrometers/pixel. The 120 adjacent-frame pairs were 
predefined using the raw acquisition sequences before any 
reconstruction results were inspected and were then held fixed for all 
methods. Raw, Wiener, and MOSR used the same images, nine 
features, scale, boundary, and circle center. 
All images retained the same contrast polarity, and no method-
specific edge cleanup was introduced. Pixel coordinates were 
converted to object space only after the circle centers had been 
estimated. Reconstruction comparisons are therefore not affected by 
different interpolation, threshold, or scale-conversion procedures. 
B. Measurement performance 
MOSR achieved the lowest aggregate value for every reported 
criterion (Table I and Fig. 2). Relative to Raw, Step SD, nominal 
MAE, and Median within-grid SD decreased by 17.9%, 18.0%, and 
61.0%. Relative to fixed Wiener, the reductions were 7.8%, 10.6%, 
and 18.2%. MOSR returned the lowest within-grid SD in all four 
imaging conditions and the lowest Step SD and nominal MAE in 
three of four conditions. At both magnifications, its Step SD 
remained near 0.06 native pixel. 
At 50x, MOSR reduced the Wiener magnification-level Step SD, 
nominal MAE, and Median within-grid SD from 4.83, 4.37, and 3.41 
nm to 4.51, 4.02, and 2.56 nm. At 20x, the corresponding values 
decreased from 10.98, 8.47, and 13.11 nm to 10.07, 7.46, and 7.55 
nm. The larger 20x nanometer values are consistent with its coarser 
sampling, while the fractional-pixel Step SD remains comparable 
across objectives. 
In a matched upsampling comparison, replacing anti-aliased 
upsampling increased Median within-grid SD by 0.27 nm, with a 
95% paired resampling interval of 0.05-0.74 nm, while changes in 
Step SD and nominal MAE remained below 0.1 nm. This localizes 
the supported role of anti-aliasing to cross-position consistency 
rather than attributing every MOSR improvement to one component. 
Across the full method, all six 95% paired baseline-minus-MOSR 
intervals remained above zero. MOSR produced lower within-grid 
SD than Raw in 119 of 120 comparisons and lower within-grid SD 
than Wiener in 92 of 120 comparisons. The conclusions concern 
comparative in-system stability rather than external length 
traceability or cross-device generalization.

IV. CONCLUSION 
MOSR combines spatial-variation-aware PSF guidance, anti-
aliased shift-stable reconstruction, and an input-anchored geometry-
preserving output. With one set of weights and one common 
measurement backend, the method achieved the best aggregate 
repeatability, nominal agreement, and within-grid consistency across 
four 20x/50x sequences and 120 predefined pairs. MOSR therefore 
improves reconstruction for cross-magnification subpixel position 
measurement rather than pursuing visual sharpness alone. 
The shared native-grid convention also preserves a common 
position definition when the inspection objective changes, supporting 
repeatable alignment and registration of periodic circular features in 
the tested system.
